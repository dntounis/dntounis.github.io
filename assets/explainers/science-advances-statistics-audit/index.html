When a small tail is not a p-value

The stored calculations reproduce. The 5% decision rule does not. Explore the difference using your actual permutation draws, an exact toy example, and the ten audited tests.

Pr(p≤α∣H0)≤α
A valid p-value produces false rejections at most α of the time when its null model and testing procedure are valid. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

Why can “extreme on both” make the score deceptively small?

Requiring a null draw to exceed both coordinates shrinks the counted region. That does not mean the observed point is rare under every sensible ordering. Imagine an observation whose marginal tails are both 10%. Under independence, only 1% of draws exceed both, so its joint score is 0.01. Yet 5.61% of null observations achieve a product score at most 0.01 somewhere in the plane.

The individual rectangle has 1% probability, but the rejection rule includes many different rectangles associated with possible observations. Calibration measures the probability of the entire rejection region. The next panel shows this distinction exactly.

Derive the correction and the 20% counterexample

Under independent uniform U and V, the raw score S = UV. For 0 < s ≤ 1, split the square at U = s. For U ≤ s, every V qualifies. For U > s, only V ≤ s/U qualifies.

FS(s)=s+∫s1sudu=s(1−lns)

At s = 0.05 the result is 0.1997866: about 200 false alarms per 1,000 null experiments, not 50. At s = 0.01 it is 0.0560517. Applying the correct continuous null CDF to S gives a uniform calibrated p-value.

For identical tails U = V, the joint tail is U itself, not U squared. Its raw 0.05 cutoff has 5% size. Dependence therefore matters. Your real null clouds also have skewness and ties; a Pearson correlation alone does not determine their calibration.

Does a calibrated 0.047 mean a 4.7% chance the finding is false?

No. It concerns how often the chosen null procedure would produce a statistic at least as extreme. It is not a posterior probability of the null, a replication probability, or proof of historical causation. In this manuscript it also does not account for every choice of threshold, pivot, corpus, or encoder.

Attention: why 0.016 → 0.047 or 0.144?

For scrambled attention, 78 of 5,000 draws exceed both absolute centered coordinates. Thus the raw score is 79/5,001 = 0.0157968. The audit finds 234 pseudo-observations with scores no larger than this, giving 235/5,001 = 0.0469906. It asks how often the score itself looks at least this small.

The uncentered max statistic instead asks whether max(|DI|, |DP|) is at least the observed 1.64014. There are 718 exceedances: 719/5,001 = 0.143771. That is a different ordering of the same draws, not a second correction to 0.047. The null mean is (−0.73289, −0.25655), so centering also makes a meaningful difference.

The defensible conclusion is that this local attention result is sensitive to the statistical definition. It is not uniformly robust evidence at 5%. A value barely below 0.05 should not determine the scientific story, especially with unresolved calibration details and data-dependent selection.

The audit calibration has a finite-sample issue

The observed score uses 5,000 reference draws, so its minimum is 1/5,001. Each leave-one-out pseudo-score uses 4,999 reference draws, so its minimum is 1/5,000. The first is strictly smaller. Consequently, every zero-exceedance observation automatically receives the audit’s minimum calibrated value; no pseudo-score can be as small.

There is also an asymmetry: pseudo-scores exclude their own draw but never include the observed point. This leave-one-out exercise is useful evidence of miscalibration, but is not an exact exchangeable calibration construction. Before publishing inferential values, use a justified symmetric augmented-sample construction, or an independent reference/calibration split, and verify its size. That is a relatively small calculation on cached draws; it does not require rerunning embeddings.

The JSON contains six calibrated-at-floor tests, not seven as the audit summary says. Attention/random remains 0.00079984. The floor issue does not prove these six signals are weak; it limits what this calibration procedure establishes.

What does “tied at the permutation floor” mean?

With 5,000 permutations, zero exceedances yields 1/5,001 ≈ 0.000200. Deep learning and generative models both hit that floor in the separate per-concept comparison. They cannot be ordered by that estimate. Sorting the tie with max|D| reintroduces the statistic that the manuscript says is not driving the ranking.

The graphical-models rival has one exceedance. A difference of zero versus one exceedance is not persuasive evidence of a stable ordering. Remove the claim that a sign-aware test independently restores deep learning to first. If retained, report the tie and tie-break explicitly. More permutations improve resolution, but cannot repair an uncalibrated test or an inappropriate null.

Priorities before submission

Keep the implemented geometry, but fix the inference before preserving the significance claims. “No need to regenerate embeddings” is supportable. “No numerical or figure changes needed” is too broad.

  1. Choose and validate the primary inferential rule. Recalibrate on the cached paired draws with a justified finite-sample procedure, or use a prespecified scalar permutation statistic. Retain alternatives as sensitivity checks. Update Table 3 and statements consistently; do not select the method because attention lands below 0.05.
  2. Test a scientifically relevant random-removal comparison. SR removes 16 assigned target papers, but random sets are drawn from 1,511 post-pivot non-target papers, only 27 of which enter geometry. The expected number removed from geometry is 16 × 27/1,511 ≈ 0.286; 75% of stored draws give zero perturbation. A calibrated test against this pool still answers a weak comparison. Separate no-op removals from undefined standardized responses. Consider assigned-paper/count and temporal matching, with feasibility stated explicitly.
  3. Separate fixed-setting and search-adjusted evidence. Local tests hold the selected pivot and threshold fixed. The existing global scan adjusts concept/year search for a different statistic, not threshold tuning. Report this limitation. Its global tail is small for SR; Gödel ≈ 0.15, deep learning ≈ 0.088 and attention ≈ 0.965 are not small. Higgs remains a known assignment/variance-collapse failure despite its nominal small probabilities.
  4. Correct definitions and legends now. Document the normalized grand mean J and its relationship to P. Remove independent radial/angular claims. Remove “temporally matched” for the implemented null, “sign-aware restores rank” and universal significance wording. Explain that Fig. 5 stars use the paired maximum absolute effect under random removal, testing only the target; an unstarred context bar is not evidence it failed a test.
  5. Add robustness and provenance; regenerate affected displays. Include the dependence-preserving bootstrap sensitivity, mark undefined grid cells as missing, record null-runner overrides and text/model conventions, and document nested ML corpora. Their stated 2005–2018 and 2005–2023 windows overlap; do not copy the audit’s “disjoint” wording.
  6. Archive the final evidence. Corrected figures and new calibration results must be deposited and cited with matching versions. A private, git-ignored audit does not make those new results available to reviewers. Retain prior versions; new results do not require deleting the original release.
Why the inertia correction is different from the statistical correction

The audit traces the actual implementation to distances from the unit-normalized grand mean. Documenting that J preserves the already-computed geometry. For unit centroids, nonzero mean m and N ≥ 2:

J=2N(1−‖m‖),P=JN−1(1−J4N)

At fixed N both summarize the same angular concentration. Removing a concept changes N and the configuration, and N can vary over time, so their ablation differences and standardized responses can differ. This is a description correction supported by reconstruction. In contrast, labeling an uncalibrated score a p-value changes the interpretation of a decision rule. To retain significance claims you need validated inference, not just a renamed column.
